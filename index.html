🎭 Finding Your Academic Voice

Your "voice" in academic writing is your unique presence as an author. It's how you position yourself in relation to your research, your field, and your readers.

"Academic writing is not about being impersonal; it's about adopting the appropriate persona for your disciplinary conversation."
– Inspired by Swales & Feak

🎯 Voice Dimensions

Hedging

Expressing uncertainty appropriately: "It appears that..."

Strong: This proves climate change is man-made.

Hedged: The evidence suggests a strong correlation between human activity and climate change patterns.

Boosting

Asserting confidence when needed: "Clearly, the data shows..."

Tentative: This might perhaps indicate something.

Boosted: These findings unequivocally demonstrate the need for immediate intervention.

Positioning

Aligning with scholarly communities

Outsider: I think researchers should...

Insider: As discourse analysts, we understand that...

📚 Research as Narrative

Every research paper tells a story. Learn to structure your work with narrative arc, character development (your research journey), and resolution (your findings).

"The research article is a story... It has a beginning (the problem), a middle (the investigation), and an end (the solution)."
– John M. Swales

📖 Narrative Structures in Research

1. The Problem-Solution Narrative

Setup: Here's a problem in our field...

Development: Previous attempts have been made...

Resolution: Our study offers a new approach...

2. The Gap-Filling Narrative

Known territory: We know a lot about X...

The gap: But we know little about Y...

The bridge: This study connects X and Y...

3. The Journey of Discovery

Starting point: We began by asking...

Challenges: We encountered difficulties with...

Insights: Unexpectedly, we discovered...

🏛️ Genre Expectations

Different academic disciplines have different "story forms." Learn the conventions of your field's genre expectations.

"Genres are not just forms, but typical ways of engaging in typical situations with typical readers."
– Swales on Genre Analysis

📊 Discipline-Specific Story Forms

Humanities & Literature

Story form: Interpretive argument

Voice: First-person acceptable

Structure: Thesis-driven essay

Social Sciences

Story form: Empirical investigation

Voice: Third-person preferred

Structure: IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion)

Natural Sciences

Story form: Experimental report

Voice: Impersonal, objective

Structure: Highly standardized sections

Applied Fields

Story form: Problem-solution

Voice: Practical, direct

Structure: Executive summary → Analysis → Recommendations

🔍 The CARS Model

Creating A Research Space: The essential framework for introducing your research effectively.

"The CARS model represents the rhetorical moves that establish a territory, create a niche, and then occupy that niche."
– John M. Swales

🗺️ CARS Model Interactive Guide

1

Establish Territory

Show the importance of your topic

→
2

Establish a Niche

Identify the gap or problem

→
3

Occupy the Niche

Present your research solution

Move 1: Territory

Strategies:

  • Claim centrality
  • Make topic generalizations
  • Review previous research

Move 2: Niche

Strategies:

  • Counter-claim
  • Indicate a gap
  • Question-raising

Move 3: Occupation

Strategies:

  • Outline purposes
  • Announce present research
  • Indicate structure
